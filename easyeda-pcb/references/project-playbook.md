# Hardware project management playbook

Use this playbook to lead a new PCB or hardware project from the initial idea through the requested build. Scale the records and reviews to the cost, risk, and maturity of the project. A prototype can use one short brief and a test list. A production build may need separate test, manufacturing, sourcing, and change records. Follow the customer's review process when one exists.

## Run the requirements interview

1. **Read what exists.** Identify the desired outcome, current stage, requested deliverables, and documents already provided. Use the latest revision of the brief, schematic, BOM, pinout, enclosure drawing, board files, and test evidence when available. Record what they establish, any contradictions, and what remains open. Carry the answers forward between turns.
2. **Start with purpose and scope.** For a sparse request, establish who uses the product, what it must do in one representative use case, what must be delivered, and the target build or date. Determine whether this is a PCB only, an assembled board, or an integrated product including mechanics, firmware, cabling, and testing. Ask who decides tradeoffs when ownership is unclear and matters.
3. **Ask the next consequential questions.** Group the smallest set of unresolved questions that affect the next decision. Two to four focused questions usually make a useful first batch. Adapt from the answers. Ask for a file or drawing when it defines an interface more reliably than a verbal description. Look up determinate component, fabricator, or interface facts from authoritative sources. Ask the user for preferences, project-specific conditions, unresolved tradeoffs, or artifacts that are missing.
4. **Maintain a working record.** Use the existing project brief or the [project record template](../assets/project-record.md). Mark applicable topics as answered, provisional, or open. Record the reason for any exclusion that affects scope. For a missing input, record its impact, who can resolve it, and the decision or milestone that needs it. Advance work independent of that input, including reversible options and tests. Keep unresolved critical facts visible when recommending a design.
5. **Read the brief back before committing dependent decisions.** Summarize the intended build, measurable requirements, hard constraints, and tradeoffs for the user. Highlight any interpretation that changes functionality, form, budget, schedule, or verification. Confirm those decisions with the responsible person when their agreement is needed. For a narrow edit or review, enter at the appropriate stage and ask only for gaps that affect it.

## Cover the applicable requirements

Use this table to find gaps. Start with the project purpose and the topics that could change architecture. Return to the remaining topics before their relevant review.

| Topic | Questions to resolve | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product and scope | Who uses the product, where, and for what job? What are the normal use, setup, shutdown, service, and foreseeable fault cases? What must the first build demonstrate? Which outputs are in scope, and what can wait? | Use cases, functional block diagram, build goal, delivery list. |
| Function and performance | Which features and modes are required? What measurable limits apply to response time, throughput, accuracy, noise, range, runtime, or other relevant behavior? At what loads and conditions? What behavior is acceptable on a fault or loss of power? | Feature priorities, acceptance criteria, worst-case conditions. |
| Power, thermal, and faults | How is the board powered? What are input limits, load envelopes, startup/shutdown, inrush, standby, and any battery or charging needs? What are the heat sources, cooling limits, protection requirements, and required isolation boundaries? | Supply and load budget, power tree, thermal assumptions, protection requirements. |
| Interfaces, firmware, and service | What connects to the PCB, at which connector and pinout, and at what electrical levels, speed, or cable length? Who owns the mating end? What boot, configuration, programming, update, debug, or service access is needed? For analog, RF, or high-speed interfaces, what device and interface constraints apply? | Interface table, pinout, cable or mating drawing, firmware responsibilities, interface test plan. |
| Mechanical and integration | What outline, mounting pattern, connector locations, component heights, insulation barriers, antenna keepouts, thermal interfaces, and tolerance limits apply? Which revision and datum define them? How will it be installed or accessed? | Dimensioned drawing or STEP file, mating parts, keepout views. |
| Environment, safety, and compliance | What operating, storage, and transport conditions apply? What environment, markets, intended use, safety requirements, and approved standards or qualification evidence govern the product? Who owns compliance decisions? | Temperature and exposure limits, product or customer specifications, applicable standards, validation plan. |
| Cost, supply, and build | What prototype and production quantities, cost targets, dates, and long-lead parts matter? What parts, alternates, fab or assembly vendors, layer count, or process options are mandatory or flexible? Who handles purchasing, assembly, fixtures, and programming? | Cost and schedule targets, sourcing notes, fabricator options, assembly and test plan. |
| Acceptance and ownership | Who evaluates the result, on what revision, and under which conditions? How will each mandatory requirement be checked? What evidence can be obtained with the available equipment, samples, and time? | Requirements and verification matrix, smoke-test plan, test owners and acceptance decisions. |

## Turn answers into design inputs

- Give each material requirement a stable ID. Write one observable requirement per entry with a target or limit, units, tolerance, operating conditions, source, and priority. Keep user needs separate from chosen implementations in the record. For example, a requirement for a measured rail voltage over a load range can be met by several regulator designs.
- Keep a short interface table for each board boundary. Record the two ends, ownership, signal direction, electrical limits, mechanical mating conditions, and relevant version. Allocate each requirement to hardware, firmware, mechanics, manufacturing, or a joint owner.
- Assign a verification method such as test, inspection, analysis, or demonstration and a pass criterion before the dependent design is finalized. Link the resulting evidence to the requirement and tested revision. Use intended-use validation to confirm the product works for its users and environment.
- Record constraints and design decisions separately from open assumptions. When requirements conflict, state the tradeoff in the terms that matter to the user, such as size, current, noise, cost, schedule, or test access. Record the decision and which requirements it affects. Keep unresolved conflicts open until resolved.
- Retain source and revision for datasheets, customer specs, regulatory requirements, quotes, and drawings. Use the [hardware reference workflow](hardware-rag.md) for vendor evidence. Use the [PCB design rulebook](design-rules.md) for geometry, stackup, and process-specific constraints.

An example requirement is “The 3.3 V rail shall stay between 3.20 V and 3.40 V at the load connector with a 4.75–5.25 V input, 20–300 mA steady-state load, and 25 °C ambient.” Plan a bench test with the specified input and load ranges, measure at the connector, and record results by board revision. These example values illustrate the format.

## Review the project at the appropriate stages

A review can be a short evidence check or a scheduled team review. Mark the outcome ready, ready with recorded limitations for the stated purpose, or held. A missing fact holds the decision that depends on it. A review remains open where a required check lacks evidence.

| Stage | Work and deliverables | Evidence to proceed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Define the product | Agree on use cases, project boundary, priorities, deliverables, milestones, and first-build purpose. Draft requirements and verification criteria. | The acceptance conditions and material constraints are clear enough for architecture. Open inputs have owners and an impact. |
| 2. Establish architecture | Draw the block diagram, power tree and board interfaces. Compare the feasible options, part availability, layer count, and fab/assembly path. Identify expensive-to-change choices and early tests for the largest risks. | The proposed approach fits the relevant targets or has explicit tradeoffs. Interface definitions, budgets, and risks support the next design decisions. |
| 3. Review detailed design | Capture the schematic and BOM. Review critical pinouts, power, clocks, boot, protection, debug access and footprints. Confirm mechanical integration, fab stackup, rules, placement and critical routing. Plan programming and test access before finishing layout. | Schematic, footprints and PCB are consistent. Review action items, applicable checks and rule exceptions are resolved or appropriately scoped to the build. Follow the [EasyEDA workflow](easyeda-workflow.md) and [layout guidance](stackup-and-layout.md). |
| 4. Release a prototype build | Freeze the revision and assembly variant. Prepare fabricator and assembler files, BOM/placement reconciliation, CAM review, and a bring-up plan with safe first-power checks. Define which features this build will exercise. | The package passes the [fabrication release checks](release-checks.md) for the intended build. Known limitations and the plan for obtaining physical evidence are recorded. |
| 5. Bring up and iterate | Identify the as-built PCB, population variant and firmware. Check rails, clocks, reset and programming before proceeding to interfaces and functional tests appropriate to the design. Log test results, failures, suspected causes and planned changes. | Evidence supports the prototype objective. Defects and untested conditions are visible. Record fixes and rerun the affected checks on the resulting revision. |
| 6. Validate and prepare production, if in scope | Verify requirements on the intended revision. Validate the product in its intended use and environment. Resolve applicable reliability, EMC, safety, and regulatory tests. Confirm manufacturing instructions, parts and approved alternates, fixtures, programming, traceability and test coverage. Use a pilot build where process evidence or yield is required. | Test and acceptance records identify the released variant. Production controls and transfer evidence meet the project's objectives. Record any authorized deviation and remaining obligations. |

For production programs, common hardware phases include **EVT** for engineering builds and integration risk, **DVT** for validation of a production-intent design, and **PVT** for validation of production processes and ramp readiness. Use the program's own definitions, quantities, and exit criteria. A one-off or proof of concept can finish once its intended prototype evidence and handoff are complete.

## Maintain scope, schedule, and revisions

- Keep the next decision, milestone, owner, and dependency visible. Work backward from committed dates and distinguish a requested date from a date supported by known lead times. Identify material schedule risks such as enclosure changes, long-lead parts, fixture development, and lab availability when relevant.
- For a material change, record what changed and why, then check the effects on requirements, schematic, layout, BOM, firmware, enclosure, supply, test, cost and schedule. Reopen only affected reviews and rerun the checks whose evidence is invalidated.
- At handoff, state the source and board revision, completed checks, accepted limitations, remaining risks, and the next action or owner. Distinguish a drafted artifact, an observed result, and a result someone still needs to obtain.

This workflow adapts public systems-engineering practices for requirements and verification and public electronics manufacturing practices for prototype, design validation and production transfer.
